== Artificial Intelligence (AI)

[cols="20%,15%,65%", options="header", stripes=even]
|===
| Term | Classification | Meaning

| AI Developer
| Role
| A software developer who builds applications and systems that integrate
AI capabilities, typically by consuming models via APIs or frameworks.
Distinct from an AI Engineer in that the focus is on application construction
rather than model development or infrastructure.

| AI Engineer
| Role
| A technical practitioner who designs, builds, and operationalizes AI
systems, spanning model selection, training pipelines, inference
infrastructure, and integration patterns. Bridges the gap between data
science and production engineering.

| AI Ethics
| Discipline
| The study and application of moral principles to the design, development,
and deployment of AI systems, addressing concerns such as fairness,
transparency, accountability, and harm prevention. It is increasingly a
governance and compliance concern for organizations deploying AI at scale.

| AI Strategist
| Role
| A practitioner responsible for defining how an organization adopts and
derives value from AI, aligning AI investments with business objectives
and risk appetite. Operates at the intersection of technology, business
strategy, and governance rather than hands-on model or application development.

| Application Programming Interface (API)
| Integration Concept
| A defined contract that allows software systems to communicate with each
other by exposing a set of operations, inputs, and outputs. In the context
of AI, APIs are the primary mechanism through which models are consumed.
A client sends a request containing a prompt or data, and the model returns
a response without the consumer needing to host or understand the underlying model.

| Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)
| Model Classification
| A hypothetical form of AI capable of performing any intellectual task that
a human can, with the same degree of generalization and adaptability across
domains. No AGI system exists today. All current AI systems are narrow,
meaning they are specialized to specific tasks or domains.

| Artificial Intelligence (AI)
| Foundational
| A broad field of computer science concerned with building systems that can
perform tasks that would typically require human intelligence. These tasks
include reasoning, learning, problem solving, understanding language, and
recognizing patterns.

| Autonomous Robots
| AI Subfield
| Physical systems that use AI to perceive their environment, make decisions,
and execute actions without continuous human control. They combine computer
vision, sensor fusion, planning algorithms, and machine learning to operate
in dynamic real-world conditions.

| Autoregressive Model
| Model Architecture
| A model that generates output sequentially, predicting each element based
on all previously generated elements. Most LLMs are autoregressive. They
generate text one token at a time, with each token conditioned on the full
preceding sequence.

| Computer Vision
| AI Subfield
| A subfield of AI concerned with enabling machines to interpret and
understand visual information from images and video. It underpins
capabilities such as object detection, facial recognition, medical imaging
analysis, and autonomous navigation.

| Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)
| Model Architecture
| A neural network architecture designed for processing grid-structured data
such as images, using convolutional layers to detect local patterns like
edges, textures, and shapes. CNNs were the dominant architecture for computer
vision tasks before transformer-based vision models became prevalent.

| Data Science
| Discipline
| An interdisciplinary field that combines statistics, programming, and domain
knowledge to extract insight and value from data. It encompasses the full
lifecycle from data collection and preparation through to modeling,
interpretation, and communication of findings.

| Dataset Engineering
| ML Lifecycle Stage
| The discipline of designing, collecting, cleaning, labeling, and maintaining
datasets used to train and evaluate AI models. Data quality directly
determines model quality. Dataset engineering is increasingly recognized
as a primary determinant of AI system performance.

| Deep Learning
| AI Subfield
| A subfield of machine learning that uses neural networks with many layers
to learn increasingly abstract representations of data. It is the technique
behind most modern AI breakthroughs including image recognition, speech
processing, and large language models.

| Diffusion Model
| Model Architecture
| A generative model that learns to produce data by training on the process
of progressively adding and then reversing noise. Diffusion models are the
architecture behind most modern image generation systems including Stable
Diffusion and DALL-E.

| EU Artificial Intelligence Act
| Regulation
| A regulatory framework enacted by the European Union that classifies AI
systems by risk level and imposes corresponding obligations on developers
and deployers, from transparency requirements for limited-risk systems to
outright prohibition of unacceptable-risk applications. It is the first
comprehensive legal framework for AI and has significant implications for
any organization building or deploying AI systems that touch EU markets.

| Final Testing & Evaluation
| ML Lifecycle Stage
| The stage of model development in which a trained model is assessed against
a held-out test dataset to measure real-world performance before deployment.
It provides an unbiased estimate of how the model will perform on data it
has never seen.

| Fine-Tuning
| ML Lifecycle Stage
| The process of further training a pre-trained foundation model on a smaller,
domain-specific dataset to adapt its behavior for a particular task or context.
Fine-tuning allows organizations to customize general-purpose models without
training from scratch, significantly reducing cost and data requirements.

| Foundation Model
| Model Classification
| A large model trained on broad, general-purpose data at significant scale,
designed to be adapted to a wide range of downstream tasks through fine-tuning
or prompting. Foundation models represent a shift where a single large training
investment produces a base that many applications and use cases build upon.

| Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)
| Generative AI Type
| A machine learning architecture in which two neural networks, a generator
and a discriminator, are trained in opposition, with the generator learning
to produce synthetic outputs convincing enough to fool the discriminator.
GANs were the dominant generative architecture for images and media before
diffusion models became prevalent.

| Generative AI
| Model Classification
| A category of AI system designed to produce new content such as text, images,
audio, code, or video by learning the underlying patterns and structure
of its training data. Large language models and GANs are both examples of
generative AI applied to different output modalities.

| Hallucination
| Model Behavior
| The phenomenon in which an AI model generates output that is fluent and
confident but factually incorrect, fabricated, or inconsistent with the
provided context. Hallucination is a fundamental risk in LLM-based systems
and must be mitigated through design patterns such as retrieval-augmented
generation, output validation, and human review.

| Hybrid Model
| Model Classification
| An AI system that combines two or more model types or approaches, such
as a neural network paired with a rules-based system, or an LLM augmented
with retrieval. Hybrid models are used when no single approach adequately
addresses all requirements of a task.

| Labeled Data
| Data Classification
| A dataset in which each example has been annotated with the correct answer
or category by a human or automated process. Labeled data is the input
required for supervised learning. The model learns by comparing its
predictions against the provided labels.

| Large Language Model (LLM)
| Model Classification
| A category of machine learning model trained on large volumes of text
data using a transformer architecture. Designed to understand, reason
over, and generate human language at scale. LLMs are the foundation of
most modern AI assistants, code generation tools, and document processing
capabilities.

| Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)
| Model Architecture
| A type of recurrent neural network designed to learn long-range dependencies
in sequential data by using gating mechanisms to control what information is
retained or discarded over time. LSTMs were the dominant architecture for
sequence modeling tasks before transformers superseded them.

| Machine Learning
| AI Subfield
| A subfield of artificial intelligence in which systems learn to perform
tasks by identifying patterns in data rather than being explicitly programmed
with rules. A machine learning system improves its performance on a task as
it is exposed to more data.

| Masked Language Model
| Model Architecture
| A model trained by randomly masking tokens in input text and learning to
predict the masked tokens from their surrounding context. BERT is the
canonical example. This training approach produces models well suited to
language understanding tasks such as classification and named entity recognition.

| Metadata
| Data Classification
| Data that describes other data, providing context such as origin, format,
size, ownership, or classification without containing the subject content
itself. In architecture, metadata is critical for data governance,
discoverability, and lineage tracking across systems.

| MNIST Database
| Dataset
| A widely used benchmark dataset containing 70,000 handwritten digit images
labeled 0 through 9, used to train and evaluate image classification models.
It is considered the "hello world" of machine learning and computer vision
education.

| Model
| Foundational
| A mathematical structure trained on data to recognize patterns and produce
outputs such as predictions, classifications, or generated content. A model
is the artifact that results from training. It encodes learned relationships
between inputs and outputs without explicitly being programmed with rules.

| Model Design
| ML Lifecycle Stage
| The process of selecting and specifying the architecture, training approach,
and evaluation criteria for an AI model before training begins. Decisions
made at this stage including architecture choice, loss function, and
data requirements have downstream consequences on capability, cost, and
deployability.

| N-Gram
| Foundational
| A contiguous sequence of n items, typically words or characters, extracted
from text, used to model language patterns statistically. N-grams were a
foundational technique in pre-neural NLP and are still used in text analysis,
spell checking, and as features in some traditional machine learning pipelines.

| Narrow AI
| Model Classification
| An AI system designed and trained to perform a specific task or a limited
range of tasks, with no ability to generalize beyond its training domain.
All AI systems in production today including LLMs are narrow AI, despite
their broad language capabilities.

| Natural Language Processing (NLP)
| AI Subfield
| A subfield of AI concerned with enabling machines to understand, interpret,
and generate human language. It underpins capabilities such as translation,
sentiment analysis, summarization, and conversational interfaces, and forms
the foundation on which LLMs are built.

| Neural Network
| Foundational
| A computational model loosely inspired by the structure of biological
brains, consisting of layers of interconnected nodes that transform inputs
into outputs through learned weights. Neural networks are the foundational
building block of deep learning and underpin most modern AI systems.

| Neural Radiance Field (NeRF)
| Model Architecture
| A technique that represents a 3D scene as a continuous function learned
by a neural network, enabling the synthesis of photorealistic novel viewpoints
from a set of 2D images. NeRFs are used in 3D reconstruction, visual effects,
and spatial computing applications.

| Open Source AI
| Model Classification
| AI systems, tools, frameworks, or models whose source code, weights, or
both are made publicly available under a license permitting inspection,
modification, and redistribution. Open source AI enables organizations to
self-host models, inspect training data and behavior, and avoid vendor lock-in.

| Open Source Model
| Model Classification
| A trained AI model whose weights are publicly released, allowing anyone
to download, deploy, and fine-tune without consuming a proprietary API.
Examples include Llama and Mistral. Open source models are a key
architectural option when data privacy, cost control, or customization
requirements cannot be met by API-based consumption.

| Post-Training
| ML Lifecycle Stage
| The set of processes applied to a pre-trained model to align its behavior
with intended use, including fine-tuning, reinforcement learning from human
feedback (RLHF), and safety evaluation. Post-training is where a general
foundation model is shaped into a product suitable for deployment.

| Pre-Training
| ML Lifecycle Stage
| The initial large-scale training of a model on broad general-purpose data,
producing a foundation model with general language or domain understanding.
Pre-training is computationally intensive and typically performed by AI
providers. Most organizations consume pre-trained models rather than
conducting pre-training themselves.

| Preliminary Evaluation
| ML Lifecycle Stage
| An early assessment of model performance conducted during or after initial
training, using a validation dataset to guide decisions about architecture,
hyperparameters, and training data before committing to final training runs.
It is distinct from final evaluation in that its purpose is to inform
development decisions rather than certify production readiness.

| Prompt Engineering
| Discipline
| The discipline of designing and refining the inputs provided to an AI model
to produce accurate, reliable, and contextually appropriate outputs. Effective
prompt engineering is an architectural concern when building AI-enabled systems
as prompt design directly affects output quality, consistency, and safety.

| Recurrent Neural Network (RNN)
| Model Architecture
| A neural network architecture designed for sequential data in which outputs
from previous steps are fed back as inputs, giving the network a form of
memory across a sequence. RNNs have been largely superseded by transformers
for most sequence tasks, though they remain in use for specific time-series
and streaming applications.

| Reinforcement Learning
| ML Paradigm
| A machine learning approach in which a model learns by taking actions in
an environment and receiving feedback as rewards or penalties, optimizing
its behavior over time to maximize cumulative reward. It is used in
robotics, game playing, and is a key technique in aligning LLM behavior
through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).

| Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
| Architecture Pattern
| A design pattern in which an LLM's response is grounded by first retrieving
relevant content from an external knowledge source, typically a vector
database, and providing that content as context in the prompt. RAG mitigates
hallucination and knowledge staleness by anchoring generation in retrieved
facts rather than relying solely on the model's trained parameters.

| Self-Supervised Learning
| ML Paradigm
| A machine learning approach in which the model generates its own labels
from the structure of unlabeled data, removing the need for human annotation.
Pre-training of LLMs uses self-supervised learning. The model learns by
predicting masked or next tokens in text, with the text itself providing
the supervision signal.

| Structured Data
| Data Classification
| Data that is organized into a predefined schema with clearly defined
fields, types, and relationships. It is stored in a format that makes it
directly queryable and processable without transformation.

| Supervised Learning
| ML Paradigm
| A machine learning approach in which a model is trained on labeled examples
where the correct output is known, learning to map inputs to outputs by
minimizing the difference between its predictions and the provided labels.
It is the most widely used machine learning paradigm, applied to tasks such
as classification, regression, and prediction.

| Traditional Machine Learning
| Model Classification
| Machine learning approaches that rely on hand-crafted features and
statistical algorithms rather than deep neural networks, including decision
trees, random forests, support vector machines, and linear regression.
Traditional ML remains appropriate for structured data problems where
interpretability, low latency, or limited training data make deep learning
impractical.

| Transformer
| Model Architecture
| A neural network architecture that processes input sequences by learning
which parts of the input to attend to when producing each part of the output,
using a mechanism called self-attention. Transformers are the architectural
foundation of all modern LLMs and have largely replaced earlier sequential
architectures such as RNNs and LSTMs for language tasks.

| Turing Test
| Foundational
| A benchmark proposed by Alan Turing in 1950 in which a human evaluator
engages in natural language conversation with both a human and a machine
without knowing which is which. If the evaluator cannot reliably distinguish
the machine from the human, the machine is considered to have passed. It
remains a conceptual reference point for human-level language ability,
though it is no longer considered a rigorous measure of intelligence.

| U-Net
| Model Architecture
| A convolutional neural network architecture originally designed for
biomedical image segmentation, characterized by a symmetric encoder-decoder
structure with skip connections that preserve spatial detail. U-Net is widely
used in image segmentation tasks and has become a core component in diffusion
model architectures for image generation.

| Unlabeled Data
| Data Classification
| A dataset in which examples have not been annotated with correct answers
or categories, representing raw data in its collected form. Unlabeled data
is the input for unsupervised learning and is far more abundant than labeled
data as it requires no human annotation effort.

| Unstructured Data
| Data Classification
| Data that does not conform to a predefined schema or data model. It
carries information but requires interpretation, transformation, or
specialized processing to extract meaning from it in a consistent way.

| Unsupervised Learning
| ML Paradigm
| A machine learning approach in which a model finds structure, patterns,
or groupings in unlabeled data without being given correct answers to learn
from. Common applications include clustering, anomaly detection, and
dimensionality reduction.

| Vector Database
| Architecture Pattern
| A database designed to store and query high-dimensional numerical
representations of data called embeddings, enabling similarity-based search
rather than exact match lookup. Vector databases are a foundational
infrastructure component in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) patterns,
where relevant content is retrieved by semantic similarity before being
passed to an LLM as context.

| Vector Embedding
| Foundational
| A numerical representation of data such as text, images, or audio as
a point in a high-dimensional space, where semantic similarity corresponds
to proximity in that space. Embeddings are the mechanism by which AI systems
make unstructured data mathematically comparable and searchable.

| Vectors
| Foundational
| An ordered list of numbers that represents a point or direction in
multi-dimensional space. In AI, vectors are the fundamental data structure
used to represent inputs, outputs, and internal model states. Embeddings
are a specific application of vectors to encode semantic meaning.
